# Dune Breaker 4.21.3 — tactical gunner and CRT fractal terrain

## Close-range gunner display

Version 4.21.2 makes the right pane a close-range targeting display on black. Its shorter axis spans 60 m rather than 170 m, giving about 2.8 times the previous zoom. The buggy remains centred without impact shake. Version 4.21.3 removes the smaller inset map box: its clipping, world projection and mouse movement now use the complete right half of the display. Only nearby car symbols, a single cursor, player-connected cables and the selected weapon's footprint are drawn. Terrain contours, rut outlines, gates, pickups, the compass and the free aiming line are removed from this pane. The driver's first-person fractal terrain, gates and pickups remain visible.

Move the mouse to select a ground point or a point on an enlarged rival symbol. The player buggy is a chevron; rivals are plain rectangles. A short status message indicates ready, aiming, blocked or unreachable shots. Physical turret turn rate, elevation, weapon range and terrain occlusion still apply. Left launches and reels, release slackens the cable, right cuts it and the wheel selects collected weapons. G switches to the original turret camera, which also retains the full-range perspective view. The tighter map deliberately focuses on nearby targets.

Both player views fill equal, full-height halves of the display, with matching instrument-card dimensions and alignment. The gunner card shows the weapon and ammunition, plus a cable load meter only while attached and one concise action hint. The selected Rut stitcher previews its actual three channels; the Dune jack previews its outer footprint. Ready/reloading footprints use the actual muzzle hit and shared terrain-placement helpers. Ground targeting and collisions continue to query the existing fractal, ruts and raised dunes, even though that geometry is omitted from the gunner display.

The gunner background skips terrain tracing and reconstruction in the existing render targets. The crisp native vector symbols, multicolour overlays, CRT glow and G camera toggle remain. Vehicle physics, weapons and existing race records are compatible.

## Vector display

Version 4.15 uses monochrome vector beams on black, then applies stationary RGB/CMY overlay zones to the complete image, including the instruments. This follows the beam-and-colour-sheet arrangement in the [Vectrex service manual](https://www.ifixit.com/Document/WQvuGEQQTTPUup5j/Vectrex-Service_Manual.pdf), with the saturated palette taken from [pnoq](https://store.steampowered.com/app/4408100/pnoq/). Colour follows screen position; it is no longer a solid car or ground material. Gate posts retain a bright white core and amber halo independently of those colour zones. Gate coverage travels in the existing beam target alpha channel, so highlighting adds no render pass or texture. UI blending preserves that coverage beneath translucent panels. Version 4.16 models the display as a curved monochrome tube behind that colour sheet. A rounded glass face, a shaded bezel lip, corner-dependent beam focus, two spatial glow scales, slight brightness drift, subpixel beam drift and faint front-glass reflections give it depth. There are no raster scanlines or RGB pixel masks. This is a display approximation rather than a cycle-accurate hardware emulator.

The live beam image is drawn independently of terrain tracing, with its dimensions bounded at 1024×768 in 4.17. The glass pass uses the same bounded resolution, followed by a single-sample upscale to the full display resolution. A quarter-width/quarter-height floating-point history integrates each 4×4 patch of those thin beams, then applies separate 18 ms and 110 ms exponential decays. It contributes only the faint broad glow and afterglow, leaving the current core crisp. Gate and ordinary emission remain separate so persistent gate light keeps its white/amber treatment. Decay follows wall time; resize, seed/camera jumps, diagnostic changes, toggling the effect and gaps over 250 ms invalidate history. Mouse hover and click coordinates use the same curved-face mapping as the shader. F8 switches to a flat clean-vector display, and `--sharp` starts that way.

The hidden-line terrain net has 2, 8 and 32 m horizontal spacing, with height sections every 0.20 and 1 m. Coverage fades when a line family is too close to resolve. Footprints come from ray differentials projected onto the local analytic tangent plane. Tangent planes are anchored to the evaluated fractal height rather than the approximate ray-stop height, reducing contour phase error at the marching tolerance. The rut-refinement pass records the lowered surface, tangent plane, depth and continuous excavation amount; the beam-resolution line pass adds 2.5 cm depth sections within the physical grooves. These are sections through the actual nine-band fractal and B-spline deformation, not a replacement mesh or a separate collision surface. Shared C++/Metal coverage functions live in `games/desert/VectorStyle.inl`.

Cars use hidden vector edges of their actual blocks and capped wheel cylinders. Face-edge widths account for projection, and the wheel caps show rings and rotating spokes. Suspension, steering, tyre size and damage still move the underlying geometry. Distant terrain silhouettes use filtered hit coverage. A beam-resolution line pass reconstructs positions and footprints from the ray-hit data, then a final pass tints white beams through fixed overlay regions. Terrain tracing retains its adaptive logical-window resolution budget.

## Alternating race formats

The default series alternates closed circuits and timed endless races against the same three physical rivals. The first circuit is three laps, followed by a three-minute distance race. The next pairs use four laps/four minutes and five laps/five minutes, then cycle. Enter on the results advances; R retries the current event. N generates another seed in the current format. `--circuit --laps N` and `--endless --duration SECONDS` set the starting event and length.

A circuit is a smooth seeded closed loop with twelve gate pairs. Its position and tangent agree across the start line. Route projection unwraps progress around the loop, while ordered swept gate crossings prevent reverse crossings or a shortcut through the start area from counting as a lap. No course reset occurs at the start line: stored tyre grooves, compaction, vehicle damage, suspension and velocity continue into the next lap. Repeated contact deepens grooves up to the physical excavation limit, and later laps query that changed surface for steering, rolling resistance, suspension and body collisions.

Endless routes combine the existing meanders with seeded noise at two larger scales. The route, original fractal terrain and anchored checkpoint posts are evaluated wherever the cars travel. The old 24-gate finish does not apply. The race stops at its configured simulation time and stores validated centreline distance, including meanders. A circuit stores elapsed time; an endless race stores distance, with separate records for seed and event length. Results remain fixed during free roaming and pausing. Fresh events and retries clear terrain deformation; individual laps and recoveries retain it.

The circuit roadbook shows the full loop. Timed events show distance and a countdown. New-mode recovery returns a car to its last validated gate, keeps damage and ruts, and holds it for five seconds while the race clock runs. The old point-to-point stage remains available through `--stage` for earlier regression tests.

The previous renderer enlarged a 120–384-pixel-wide image. It also stopped the terrain at a 12 m noise-cell scale, so neither sharp edges nor a finer scale hierarchy could be seen. Version 4.3 removes that image cap and extends the actual geometry from six to nine octaves.

The default window is 1024×640. A 2× Retina display presents a 2048×1280 image from a 1024×640 beam target. Normal terrain tracing starts at 320 pixels wide and adapts up to 512 pixels using the 90th percentile of recent frame work, excluding the intentional pacing wait. All nine geometry bands are evaluated at every ray hit. F5 uses 75% of that current ray budget, with a 240-pixel floor. The ray count can change without creating textures: buffers reserve the maximum dimensions for the current window, and each pass uses an active viewport within them. Still scenes keep their current grid and refine one of eight strips at a time.

The hit buffers store full-precision tangent-plane coefficients, distance and a continuous rut-depth tag. The beam pass interpolates only valid terrain samples, intersects the resulting plane at each beam pixel and draws the original multiscale net and height contours there. This avoids magnifying low-resolution line pixels or interpolating sky into the surface. It retains all nine geometry bands rather than dropping octaves. Silhouette coverage is interpolated and antialiased separately.

Cars and checkpoint capsules are intersected analytically at beam resolution, in screen rectangles bounded by their actual shapes. A depth attachment resolves object overlap; the beam pass compares object range with reconstructed terrain range. Checkpoints no longer constrain every terrain marching step. Tyre-track occupancy is dilated in a small local array rather than repeating nine map lookups per tile. Neither change alters the physical surface or collision solver.

The macOS frame pacer follows CoreVideo's display clock, selecting a cadence near 60 Hz. Moving a window to another display updates the clock source. Metal's additional presentation wait is disabled so the two clocks do not compete. Other platforms use the precise deadline fallback. The main game thread requests high scheduling priority. `--uncapped` bypasses pacing for measurement. `--benchmark` temporarily keeps the test window above other windows, avoiding the Metal covered-window fallback during measurements. Core focus uses three beam samples, and near/broad glow reuses the existing quarter-size phosphor buffer, reducing the CRT pass from fourteen to eight texture samples. The two wall-time decay constants and fixed colour overlays are retained. The presentation swap chain has no multisampling or depth buffer because its fullscreen pass already receives antialiased vectors.

The terrain remains a direct mathematical heightfield. Vector coverage shows its actual surface sections and wire net on black. Native and CPU diagnostics share the coverage mathematics. The route neither flattens nor otherwise reshapes the terrain; tyres alter it through contact. Compaction and rut depth still affect grip and rolling resistance.

## Fractal geometry

The terrain uses nine deterministic noise evaluations. A coarse 192 m field makes basins and smooth banks between terrace levels at 0, 12, 24 and 36 m. Translating its sampling origin avoids tying race starts to a stationary point on the noise grid. A second field produces squared ridges, elongated by a factor of about 2.86 in the prevailing wind direction. Seven finer ridged bands run from a 96 m cell scale down to 1.5 m; their amplitudes begin at 1.65 m and fall by 0.55 per band.

A smooth regional gain between 0.25 and 1 multiplies the dunes and fine ridges. Low country therefore includes smoother basins, while high country has stronger broken relief. This changes actual surface height and normals. No material texture, colour mask or route-shaped terrain is used to simulate those forms.

`include/infinity/FractalField.inl` is compiled as C++ for collision and embedded into Metal for rendering. The complete analytic height is below 52 m, and its slope bound is below 2.4. A height residual scaled by 0.38 is a conservative distance bound. With ruts, the scale becomes 0.18 to include the extra groove-wall slopes. Surface normals differentiate the terrace remap, regional gain and every noise band, including product-rule terms.

The GPU interval cache bounds the terrace and relief ranges together. Its curvature calculation includes the nonlinear ridge shape, terrace transitions and the regional gain's product-rule terms. A curvature bound of 3 remains valid for general ray advances. Upper-space shortcuts begin above the new terrain and marker height bounds rather than retaining the previous low ceiling.

The heightfield supports continuous off-road driving. It has one height at each horizontal point and consequently has no caves or overhangs. The underlying weighted noise series follows the approach described in [PBRT's noise chapter](https://www.pbr-book.org/3ed-2018/Texture/Noise), applied to geometry rather than appearance.

## Tyre tracks and terrain deformation

Every loaded tyre deposits a swept contact patch into a sparse world-space sand layer at 0.125 m node spacing. Rolling distance, wheel load and longitudinal/lateral sliding determine excavation and compaction. Airborne tyres leave no marks. Repeated passes deepen existing grooves, with excavation bounded at 0.32 m. Standing wheelspin can dig a hole; ordinary stationary tyres do not.

Version 4.11 integrates the quartic elliptical contact footprint exactly along each swept tyre segment. It clips the integral to the footprint support and uses a centered polynomial integral to avoid cancellation at the ends. A long movement therefore deposits the same soil dose as the identical movement split into short steps; it no longer applies a maximum-strength stamp everywhere along that segment. Load scales the dose, and rolling and shearing contribute independently.

Excavation and compaction approach their bounds exponentially. This makes the result independent of how a fixed dose is subdivided and prevents abrupt clipping as sand compacts. The digging rate is calibrated so a normal 10 cm segment produces approximately twice the previous depth. At full depth, a node is 32 cm below the original surface. Saturated nodes retain their revision when their values stop changing, avoiding needless texture uploads. Invalid loads and recovery jumps are rejected before they can alter the soil.

The surface adds a cubic B-spline deformation field to the nine-octave fractal. CPU and Metal use the same weights and derivatives. Collision rays, body contacts, terrain normals and wheel suspension all query this lowered surface. Vector depth contours expose the same physical grooves; compaction slightly increases available grip. Deep grooves increase rolling drag. Checkpoint posts stay anchored to the original terrain, with solid foundations extending below the full excavation depth in both rendering and body collisions.

Sparse tiles remain in memory throughout the race, including after recovery and travel outside the rendered area. Restarting or regenerating the course clears them. Tracks are not saved between app sessions. Metal streams changed tiles into a moving 256 m window and fades its outer 4 m; distant marks remain stored and load again when approached. An occupancy mask avoids sixteen-node interpolation on untouched ground. The original terrain interval bounds remain conservative because excavation only removes material. The global deformation curvature bound rises to 96, and the collision residual scale is 0.18 in 4.12. Positive B-spline weights and bounded node differences give a rut-gradient bound below 2.716; the combined-terrain distance scale remains conservative when combined with the fractal slope. The local GPU curvature bounds and the upload-window fade bounds also account for the deeper geometry.

The renderer first intersects the original fractal, then uses a separate pass to refine pixels whose first hit lies in a rut. Since excavation only removes solid material, the original first hit is a conservative starting point. The refined intersection still uses the combined fractal and deformation field. Local spline curvature bounds constrain those steps within each cell, keeping detailed work away from untouched terrain.

## Acceleration without replacing the surface

The GPU maintains a scrolling 2048×2048 interval cache at 0.75 m spacing. Each entry stores a proven upper bound on terrain height throughout its world cell, together with a curvature bound. It is computed from the noise ranges over the cell, rather than from a smoothed height sample. Border refreshes use compact 8×256-cell tiles, normally one per frame and at most two when work has accumulated. While refreshes are pending, only the intersection of fully valid cache windows is sampled; rays elsewhere evaluate the original field directly. Coarse bounds are refreshed with rounded-up rectangles and are exposed only when the window is complete. Seed changes and large jumps initialize a complete cache. A dedicated test verifies 2,256,768 cell mappings across movement, reversals, toroidal wrapping, teleports and seed changes. A second 256×256 layer groups 8×8 cells with maximum bounds. Only newly exposed strips are generated when the camera moves.

Rays skip empty cells and use bounded quadratic advances near grazing ridges. Final intersections and normals still evaluate the full analytic fractal. The cache is never used as the visible surface. Conservative coarse ray cones provide another empty-space bound. Functional checkpoint posts cap those advances and sit on their actual generated ground heights.

Each vehicle consists of one sharp rectangular block and four plain capped cylinders. There are no drivers, cages, cockpits, hubs, treads or small body parts. The front wheels steer and all four wheels move with the suspension. Damage changes the block dimensions and tyre radii. Exact box and cylinder ray intersections query the same zero sets as the shared vehicle distance field.

Still scenes reuse their unchanged image and update one of eight strips per frame. Camera, frustum, terrain seed or vehicle geometry changes exit that mode immediately. Moving views do not reuse pixels from a different camera position. This saves work without lowering still-image resolution.

## Vehicle contacts and recovery

The simulation and body contact solver both run at 120 Hz. Each tyre queries five points on the actual fractal surface, accounts for its rounded rolling profile, and combines the supporting normals over its contact patch. Lateral probes now reach 0.18 m toward either tyre edge, so the cylinder can support itself on groove shoulders. Normal weighting concentrates support near the highest contacting parts of the patch. Suspension damping uses contact-point velocity, including chassis rotation, rather than differentiating clamped wheel travel. Anti-roll transfer is clamped to the load available on either side, preserving total axle load.

Compression and rebound damping are independently tuned and bypass more force at high shaft speeds. A progressive compression damper engages near the travel limit alongside the spring bump stop, dissipating energy before bottoming out. Reset and recovery first estimate the body's support plane, then fit it using the same five-point tyre queries as driving. This includes groove shoulders, rounded rolling profiles, damaged tyre radii, suspension stiffness and front/rear static load distribution. It prevents a car from being positioned at the rut's center depth and then springing upward onto its shoulders.

The block's faces, edges and corners supply 42 body samples against the same fractal field. Their sweeps include chassis rotation. Checkpoint capsules additionally query the entire box and each capped wheel cylinder, so a thin post can no longer pass between surface samples. Rotating and translating vehicles advance conservatively to first contact.

Car bodies use all 15 separating axes of the oriented boxes. Clipped face and edge contacts produce a manifold, solved through ten accumulated-impulse iterations with restitution and friction. Exposed wheel contacts against other vehicles use samples on their actual cylindrical rims. Penetration correction changes positions separately from physical velocities. The terrain and wheel-contact sampling remain finite approximations; these are not exact intersections of arbitrary fractal solids. Terrain rendering has a pixel-sized convergence tolerance; collision queries use a smaller fixed tolerance.

Gravity, spring/damper support and air resistance predict chassis motion before the tyres are solved. An eight-pass impulse solve couples all four tyre patches to the chassis's six degrees of freedom and the four wheel rotors. Each tyre uses a two-axis effective-mass matrix, including rotational inertia and the effect of off-centre forces. Braking and cornering share a load-sensitive friction circle, with a modest grip reduction after breakaway. Damage reduces both available grip and tyre stiffness.

Brake torque is finite. The solver first considers a stationary rotor, then checks whether the brake can supply the torque that contact would require. If it cannot, the rotor turns and the contact is solved with its spin inertia. This replaces the old decision to lock a tyre before its contact reaction was known. Sand rolling resistance is a resisting rotor torque transmitted through the same contact, including the greater resistance of deep ruts. Doubling the rut limit does not halve resistance at an unchanged physical depth; the added rolling-resistance coefficient still rises at the same rate per metre and can now reach 0.13 above the clean-sand coefficient of 0.035. It cannot independently arrest the chassis when grip is unavailable.

Engine braking also uses bounded resisting rotor torque. The previous signed drive-torque implementation could accelerate a backwards-spinning rotor in a forward gear, or continue driving a slowing rotor through zero. The corrected overrun torque opposes either direction and stops at zero. Regression cases exercise both directions with airborne tyres, isolating this behaviour from ground reactions.

Moving tyres use longitudinal and cornering stiffness. As rolling speed falls to zero, contact becomes static grip instead of a lateral force that needs persistent sideways creep. Chassis and rotor velocity changes are fed into subsequent patches within the same step. There is no separate delayed slip-angle force that can keep pushing in an outdated direction after the contact motion reverses. Suspension load, contact normals, compaction and rut geometry remain inputs to these contacts.

A critically damped steering rack follows the requested angle with a speed-dependent angular-rate limit. Changes in vehicle speed affect its target, without snapping the rack to a new angle. Front-tyre lateral forces feed a bounded pneumatic self-alignment response back into steering. Ackermann geometry uses the resulting physical rack angle. Wheelspin, suspension damage, alignment, load transfer and airborne motion remain physical inputs to handling. The tyres remain a ray-contact approximation: there is no separate unsprung wheel mass or deformable rubber carcass simulation.

The first-person camera is centered above the block, with no hands or steering overlay. C selects the chase camera. An overturned vehicle temporarily uses the external camera so the view stays outside the solid body and ground.

R remains an explicit player recovery with damage retained and a five-second cost. Demo automation uses that same action if it has come to rest inverted or misses a gate in the new formats, then chooses a more cautious alternate line. It does not apply an automatic upright torque during a tumble. Normal player driving is unaffected by the demo policy.

Rivals in circuits and timed events also recover to their last validated gate after missing a gate or coming to rest overturned, retaining damage and waiting five seconds. This prevents an inverted block from permanently blocking the route. Automated drivers slow down for large heading errors before turning back toward their line.

Up to 32 fixed steps run per rendered frame, with wall-time catch-up capped at 0.25 seconds. Sustained frame rates below approximately 4 FPS or longer stalls can still slow simulation time. Current physics records use `best-412-circuit-<laps>laps-<seed>.txt`, `best-412-endless-<duration>s-<seed>.txt` and `best-412-stage-<seed>.txt`, preserving earlier records separately.
